# Electrolytes and Hydration: A Scientific Report

**An independent, source-linked review of sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium and
chloride in exercise, heat and illness, with a 22-product composition and cost database.**

Prepared November 2026. No brand funding, sponsorship or affiliate links. Prices are
snapshots and will change.

---

## 1. Summary

Electrolyte products occupy a strange position in the supplement market: they are
chemically simple, physiologically real, and commercially oversold. Sodium genuinely
matters for fluid balance. Potassium genuinely matters for blood pressure. Magnesium and
calcium genuinely matter for the body, but not at the doses in a sports drink, and not
for the reasons the labels imply.

The evidence supports a narrow set of uses:

- replacing sodium during prolonged exercise or heavy sweating,
- improving fluid retention when rehydrating,
- treating dehydration from diarrhoea with oral rehydration solution,
- and supporting athletes who cannot eat during long events.

It does not support the broader claims: that everyone needs electrolytes, that sodium
prevents cramps, that potassium in a bottle improves hydration, or that a premium stick is
physiologically superior to a cheap sachet of salts.

The most important safety finding is counter-intuitive. In healthy athletes, the common
danger is not too little sodium. It is **too much water**: exercise-associated
hyponatraemia, which has killed marathon runners, hikers and military trainees. The second
is **too much potassium** in people whose kidneys or drugs cannot excrete it. Both are
preventable with simple rules.

## 2. What the five electrolytes do

### 2.1 Sodium

Sodium is the main extracellular cation. It sets plasma osmolality, determines
extracellular fluid volume, maintains blood pressure, and provides the gradient for nerve
and muscle action potentials. It also powers intestinal sodium-glucose cotransport, the
mechanism that makes oral rehydration therapy work.

Sweat sodium averages about **42 mmol/L** across 1,944 tests, with a range of 17–106
mmol/L ([evidence PH-01](../docs/generated/evidence-tables.md)). At 40 mmol/L, a litre of sweat
contains about **920 mg sodium**, or 2.3 g of salt. A heavy sweater at 70 mmol/L loses
about 1,610 mg/L; a light sweater at 20 mmol/L loses about 460 mg/L.

The adequate intake for adults is 1,500 mg/day; the chronic disease risk reduction intake
is 2,300 mg/day; WHO recommends under 2,000 mg/day. Typical US intake is 3,400–3,600
mg/day. Most people are not sodium-deficient.

### 2.2 Potassium

About 98% of body potassium is intracellular. It sets the resting membrane potential, so
small serum changes can cause arrhythmias. Higher intake lowers blood pressure and is
associated with lower stroke risk.

Sweat potassium is about **6 mmol/L** (range 3–8), roughly a quarter of the sodium
concentration. A litre of sweat contains about 230 mg potassium. A normal meal replaces it.

The adequate intake is 3,400 mg/day for men and 2,600 mg/day for women; WHO suggests at
least 3,510 mg/day from food. There is no upper intake level for healthy adults, because
healthy kidneys excrete excess, a fact that reverses in chronic kidney disease.

### 2.3 Magnesium

Magnesium is a cofactor for more than 300 enzymes, including ATP-using reactions, and it
opposes calcium-driven muscle contraction. Sweat losses are under 1 mmol/L. The RDA is
400–420 mg/day for men and 310–320 mg/day for women; the supplemental upper limit is
350 mg/day, above which diarrhoea is likely.

### 2.4 Calcium

Calcium drives excitation-contraction coupling in muscle, neurotransmitter release, hormone
secretion and blood clotting, and it mineralises bone. Sweat losses are under 1 mmol/L. The
RDA is 1,000–1,200 mg/day; the upper limit is 2,000–2,500 mg/day.

### 2.5 Chloride

Chloride is the main extracellular anion, following sodium to preserve electroneutrality.
It maintains osmolality and acid-base balance, forms gastric acid, supports the chloride
shift in red blood cells, and is required for sweat production. Sweat chloride averages
**32 mmol/L** and tracks sodium.

Chloride is the nutrient labels most often omit: only **8 of 22** products in this database
declare an amount, despite it being the second most abundant ion in sweat.

## 3. Sweat and fluid balance

Sweat is produced by eccrine glands that secrete an isotonic precursor and reabsorb sodium
and chloride in the duct. The result is hypotonic fluid: water with some salt. Whole-body
sweat rate typically ranges from **0.5 to 2.0 L/h**, and can exceed 3 L/h in extreme heat or
large athletes.

Sweat sodium varies six-fold between individuals and by 30–50% within the same person
across conditions. It falls with heat acclimation (by roughly 9–23 mmol/L, about 15 mmol/L
at matched sweat rate), rises with high dietary sodium, rises with exercise intensity, and
differs by collection site and method.

The practical consequence is that **"salty sweater" is a real phenotype but a poorly
measured one**. Patch tests have substantial error. They are useful for a rough category (light, typical, salty, very salty) and poor for prescribing a precise milligram target.

Fluid balance has a narrow safe band. Losing about **2% of body mass** is where endurance
performance commonly starts to decline and cardiovascular strain rises. Gaining weight
during exercise is the strongest modifiable risk factor for hyponatraemia. The target is to
finish within about 2% of starting weight, not to match losses exactly.

Sodium and water are one decision. Water without sodium is retained poorly; sodium without
water does not hydrate. Post-exercise, replacing 100% of the deficit with plain water still
leaves a deficit, because some is excreted. Replacing **125–150%** with a sodium-containing
fluid restores net balance more reliably.

## 4. Exercise performance, heat and endurance

The performance hierarchy is fluid, then carbohydrate, then sodium, then everything else.

**Fluid.** Dehydration raises heart rate and core temperature for the same workload and
impairs performance above about 2% body mass loss. The effect is larger in heat and at
higher intensity.

**Carbohydrate.** 30–60 g/h improves performance in events lasting 1–2.5 hours; up to
90 g/h of multiple transportable carbohydrates is recommended beyond 2.5–3 hours. A 6–8%
solution is the practical compromise between fueling and gastric emptying. Above about 8%,
emptying slows and gastrointestinal distress becomes more likely.

**Sodium.** Sodium increases thirst and voluntary intake, improves palatability, and
improves fluid retention when intake matches losses. It does **not** improve performance
when fluid intake is matched, and it does **not** prevent cramps. The 2025 review of sodium
intake for athletes concludes that sodium influences performance only insofar as it
produces more optimal fluid intake.

**Heat acclimation.** Seven to fourteen days of progressive heat exposure lowers core
temperature and heart rate, expands plasma volume, raises sweat rate and lowers sweat
sodium. It is the most effective intervention for hot-weather performance, and it is free.

**Cooling.** Ice slushies, cold towels and cold-water immersion reduce thermal strain.
They outperform any electrolyte product.

**Salt tablets.** They do not prevent heat illness. Heat illness is a cooling failure.
Exertional heat stroke requires immediate cold-water immersion.

## 5. Muscle cramps

Exercise-associated muscle cramps are the strongest test of the electrolyte story, and the
story fails.

The dehydration/electrolyte hypothesis is intuitive but poorly supported. A systematic
review found that support came mainly from anecdotal reports and small case series, while
four prospective cohort studies did not support it. A prospective study of ultramarathon
runners found no relationship between total sodium intake and cramping over races up to 30
hours. Cramping, nausea, hyponatraemia and dehydration were all unrelated to sodium intake.

The leading explanation is **altered neuromuscular control**: fatigue makes muscle spindle
and Golgi tendon organ afferents hyperexcitable, producing sustained motor neuron firing.
This explains why cramps occur in fatigued muscle, why stretching helps, and why they occur
in cool conditions.

What helps:

- stretching and massage for the acute cramp,
- reducing fatigue and progression of load,
- strength training,
- pickle juice or other sharp tastes for acute relief (small evidence, plausible
  trigeminal reflex, about 85 seconds versus 134 in one trial).

What does not:

- sodium, potassium or magnesium as prophylaxis,
- salt tablets,
- "cramp" products marketed on electrolyte balance.

There is one legitimate edge case: if an athlete is dehydrated and then drinks a large
volume of plain water, plasma sodium falls rapidly and cramp susceptibility rises. An oral
rehydration solution prevented that in a small laboratory study. That is an argument
against dilute overdrinking, not an argument for routine salt loading.

## 6. Recovery

Recovery requires fluid, sodium, carbohydrate, protein and sleep. Electrolytes contribute
to the first two and nothing meaningful to the rest.

Replacing 125–150% of the fluid deficit with sodium restores net balance. Sodium-containing
drinks reduce urine loss compared with water. A meal with salt does the same job and brings
carbohydrate and protein.

Glycogen resynthesis depends on carbohydrate and total energy, not electrolytes. Muscle
soreness is not reduced by electrolyte drinks. Hangover claims have no controlled support.
The most effective recovery interventions remain food, fluid, sleep and time.

## 7. The product landscape

Twenty-two products across seven categories were analysed. The central finding is that
**format does not predict composition**.

| Category | Sodium per serving | Cost per 1,000 mg sodium |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral rehydration solution | 490–1,725 mg | $0.23–$5.24 |
| Electrolyte powder | 400–1,120 mg | $0.93–$3.50 |
| Electrolyte drink (RTD) | 700 mg | $4.76 |
| Sports drink | 270–400 mg | $4.39–$8.81 |
| Electrolyte capsule | 80–215 mg | $1.16–$5.20 |
| Electrolyte tablet | 300 mg | $2.50 |
| Enhanced water | 0–30 mg | n/a–$239 |

Sodium concentration at the label's dilution ranges from 0 mg/L (CORE) to 2,368 mg/L
(Waterboy). Against average sweat at about 966 mg/L, that is 0× to 2.5×.

### 7.1 Powders and sticks

The dominant direct-to-consumer format. Flexible, high sodium per serving, and the most
expensive per milligram in the mid-tier. A 6 g stick holding 1,000 mg sodium is mostly
salt plus flavour.

### 7.2 Tablets

Cheap per serving, weak per dose. A 300 mg tablet replaces about 300 mL of average sweat.
Reasonable for flavour and light replacement; inadequate for heavy sweating.

### 7.3 Ready-to-drink sports drinks

270–400 mg sodium and 25–36 g carbohydrate per bottle. Designed for palatability and fuel,
not sweat replacement. The worst value per milligram because you are paying for water and
packaging.

### 7.4 Oral rehydration solutions

The clinical benchmark: 75 mmol/L sodium, 20 mmol/L potassium, 65 mmol/L chloride, 10
mmol/L citrate, 75 mmol/L glucose, 245 mOsm/L. The glucose drives sodium-glucose
cotransport. ORS is the cheapest sodium in the database and the correct treatment for
diarrhoea. It is not a sports drink.

### 7.5 Capsules

Sodium without fluid. Useful when carrying water is difficult; risky without enough water.
SaltStick is the best value at 215 mg per capsule; Hammer Endurolytes is only 80 mg per
two-capsule serving.

### 7.6 Enhanced waters

Where "electrolyte" means the least. CORE declares no sodium and no amounts for its
minerals. PRIME and BODYARMOR are potassium-forward with 10–30 mg sodium. They are
flavoured waters with marketing, not sweat replacement.

## 8. Composition analysis

### 8.1 Sodium is the dose that matters

The useful comparison is sodium per litre against sweat. Most electrolyte products are
stronger than sweat; most sports drinks are weaker. Neither is inherently wrong, but the
label does not tell you which you are buying.

### 8.2 Sodium-to-potassium ratio

Sweat is sodium-dominant: about 42 mmol/L sodium against 6 mmol/L potassium, a molar ratio
near 7:1. Products range from 13.6:1 (Skratch) to 1.7:1 (Nuun Rest), with potassium-dominant
outliers (PRIME, BODYARMOR) that are the wrong direction for hydration. A 700 mg potassium
drink replaces the electrolyte you lost least.

### 8.3 Carbohydrate

Carbohydrate concentration determines function. 6.1% (Gatorade) fuels and hydrates; 3.8%
(Skratch) is a light fuel; 2.5% (Liquid I.V.) is hydration with a little sugar; 0% (Re-Lyte)
is hydration only. For events over 2.5 hours, 90 g/h cannot come from a 6% drink without
drinking 1.5 L/h, so long-course athletes combine drinks with gels and bars.

### 8.4 Sugar-free and sweeteners

Sugar-free is reasonable for daily hydration and short sessions, and wrong for endurance
fueling. Stevia, sucralose and acesulfame K have no established performance harm at typical
doses. Erythritol's cardiovascular association is hypothesis-generating, not causal; EFSA
found no causal relationship. Aspartame is IARC Group 2B on limited evidence, with the
acceptable daily intake unchanged. WHO advises against non-sugar sweeteners for weight
control, which is a different question from whether a sugar-free drink is safe.

### 8.5 Additives

Citric acid, colours, preservatives, vitamins and BCAAs are present at food doses and are
not safety concerns. Caffeine is a genuine ergogenic but can worsen anxiety and sleep.
Proprietary blends are unevaluable and should be avoided.

### 8.6 Label transparency

Sodium and potassium are declared by 21 of 22 products; magnesium by 13; calcium by 6;
chloride by 8. Chloride is the clearest gap. A product that lists "electrolytes" without
amounts is not giving the consumer actionable information.

## 9. Safety

### 9.1 Sodium

Reducing sodium lowers blood pressure by about 4.3/2.1 mmHg per 130 mmol reduction, in
hypertensive and normotensive people. DASH-Sodium found an 11.5 mmHg systolic reduction in
hypertensive participants from the combination of DASH and 1,500 mg sodium. Long-term
follow-up of TOHP found about 25% fewer cardiovascular events with sodium reduction. PURE's
J-shaped curve is largely a measurement artefact of spot urine.

A 1,000 mg sodium stick contains about 2.5 g of salt, half the WHO daily limit. One is not
a problem for a healthy active person; repeated use on top of a high-salt diet is a
meaningful load.

### 9.2 Potassium

The risk is excretion, not intake. Healthy kidneys handle high potassium; CKD, heart
failure, adrenal insufficiency, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs, trimethoprim and NSAIDs impair
excretion and can cause fatal hyperkalaemia. Potassium-enriched salt reduced stroke by 14%
in a large trial, but it is contraindicated in advanced CKD. FDA limits OTC potassium drug
products to 99 mg per dose because of small-bowel lesions; supplements are not bound by
that limit.

### 9.3 Magnesium and calcium

Supplemental magnesium above 350 mg/day causes diarrhoea; in kidney failure it can cause
hypermagnesaemia. High-dose calcium supplements have a contested cardiovascular signal.
Neither belongs in a hydration product at a meaningful dose.

### 9.4 Medication interactions

The clinically important ones are potassium-raising drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs,
trimethoprim) and lithium, whose levels shift with sodium and water intake. A stable sodium
and fluid intake matters more than an "optimal" one for people on lithium.

### 9.5 Who should not use these products without advice

CKD, dialysis, heart failure, hyperkalaemia history, adrenal insufficiency, potassium-
restricted diets, pregnancy with hypertension, children under 12 (except ORS for diarrhoea),
and anyone on the drugs above.

## 10. Dehydration and overhydration

**Dehydration** is a body-water deficit. At 2% body mass loss, performance begins to fall;
at 5–6%, heat exhaustion risk rises; above 7% is an emergency. Treatment is oral fluid with
sodium if the person can drink, and intravenous fluid if they cannot.

**Exercise-associated hyponatraemia** is the opposite problem and the more dangerous one.
In the 2002 Boston Marathon, 13% of finishers had hyponatraemia and 0.6% had critical
hyponatraemia. The cause is drinking more hypotonic fluid than the kidneys can excrete,
often with vasopressin stimulation. Weight gain during exercise, fluid intake over 3 L,
duration over 4 hours, slow pace and low BMI are risk factors. Sodium intake may attenuate
the fall in blood sodium but cannot prevent EAH when fluid intake is excessive.

Treatment of severe EAH is a **100 mL bolus of 3% NaCl intravenously**, repeated up to twice
at 10-minute intervals, aiming to raise serum sodium by 4–5 mmol/L or until symptoms
resolve. It is a medical emergency.

**Hypernatraemia** is usually a water deficit, not a salt excess. Salt tablets without water
can cause it, but that is rare.

**Exertional heat stroke** is a cooling emergency. Cold-water immersion is definitive;
fluids are adjunctive.

## 11. Brands, testing and cost

Only a minority of products in this sample hold third-party sport certification. Precision
PH 1000 is NSF Certified for Sport; PRIME and DripDrop Zero appear in NSF listings for some
SKUs. Certification is product- and lot-specific, not brand-wide, and it says nothing about
formulation quality.

Transparency varies more than quality. Nuun, SaltStick, Re-Lyte and TRIORAL disclose the
most; CORE discloses the least.

Cost per 1,000 mg sodium ranges from **$0.23** (TRIORAL ORS) to **$8.81** (Gatorade), a
**38×** spread. The electrolyte itself costs about a tenth of a cent per dose. The rest is
flavour, packaging, convenience and brand.

Value tiers:

- **Budget:** TRIORAL, Re-Lyte, SaltStick.
- **Mid:** LMNT, NormaLyte, Waterboy, Nuun, Liquid I.V., Skratch, Pedialyte, DripDrop, PH
  1000.
- **Premium:** Gatorade Zero, BIOLYTE, Pedialyte RTD, Powerade, Gatorade.

Premium is not a chemical category. It is a marketing one.

## 12. Dosage guidance

Set three numbers: fluid, sodium and carbohydrate.

**Fluid.** Measure sweat rate by weighing in and out. Replace 60–80% during exercise and the
rest afterwards; finish within 2% of starting weight.

**Sodium.** Estimate loss as sweat volume × concentration × 23 mg/mmol. Replace 40–70%
during exercise. Typical targets: 300–600 mg/h for 1–2.5 hours; 700–1,200 mg/h for heavy
sweaters in heat. Split doses; avoid single large boluses.

**Carbohydrate.** 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 hours; 60–90 g/h beyond 2.5 hours, using multiple
transportable carbohydrates.

**Before exercise.** 5–10 mL/kg in the 2–4 hours before. Add sodium at 275–420 mg/100 mL if
retention is a problem.

**After exercise.** 125–150% of the deficit over 2–4 hours, with sodium and a meal.

**Do not:** take salt tablets without water, chase a fixed sweat-test number, drink to a
schedule that causes weight gain, use sports drinks for diarrhoea, assume "electrolyte"
means sodium, or use potassium-heavy products with kidney disease or potassium-raising
drugs.

---

## 13. Verdict

Electrolyte supplements are useful for prolonged exercise, heat, heavy sweating, diarrhoea
and post-exercise rehydration. For ordinary living and exercise under an hour, water and
food are sufficient.

Sodium is the electrolyte that matters acutely. Potassium matters for blood pressure over
years. Magnesium, calcium and chloride in sports drinks are irrelevant at the dose. The
real risks are overdrinking and potassium excess in at-risk people, not sodium deficiency
in athletes.

The best-value products are unglamorous: an ORS sachet at $0.23 per 1,000 mg sodium, a
Re-Lyte scoop at $0.93, a SaltStick capsule at $1.16. The premium stick is a convenience,
not a pharmacological upgrade.

If you remember one thing: **weigh yourself, drink to thirst, and do not drink more than
you lose.**

## 14. Methodology and limitations

This review synthesised randomised trials, systematic reviews, cohorts, consensus
statements, guidelines and regulatory documents, and built a 22-product database normalised
to sodium per serving and cost per 1,000 mg. Labels were not independently assayed; prices
are November 2026 snapshots; the product sample is purposive, not a census. The full method,
grading scheme, search strategy and limitations are in
[the methodology chapter](../docs/01-methodology.md). Every quantitative table is generated
from the CSV datasets by `analysis/build_tables.py`, and CI fails if a committed table is
stale.
