# Exercise performance, heat and endurance

## The performance hierarchy

Not all ingredients in a sports drink matter equally. Ranked by evidence:

1. **Fluid**: preventing deficits above ~2% body mass protects performance, especially
   in heat.
2. **Carbohydrate**: 30–90 g/h improves endurance performance in events over about an
   hour, with the benefit growing with duration.
3. **Sodium**: helps only insofar as it improves fluid intake or retention. It is not an
   independent ergogenic.
4. **Potassium, magnesium, calcium**: no established performance effect at the doses in
   sports products.

That ranking explains why a cheap carbohydrate-electrolyte drink can outperform an
expensive low-carbohydrate "electrolyte" stick in a long event, and why a high-sodium
stick can be useful in heat even though sodium is not a performance drug.

## Dehydration and performance

Dehydration raises cardiovascular strain: plasma volume falls, stroke volume falls, heart
rate rises, and core temperature climbs for the same workload
([HY-02](generated/evidence-tables.md), [HY-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). At about
2% body mass loss, endurance performance begins to decline; at 3–5% the decline is
substantial, particularly in heat ([HY-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

The relationship is not a cliff. Trained athletes can perform well while mildly
dehydrated, especially in cool conditions, and some evidence suggests that drinking to
complete euhydration in a marathon is unnecessary and possibly counterproductive. The
defensible target is to avoid getting badly behind, not to match losses exactly.

## Carbohydrate: the part that actually improves performance

The American College of Sports Medicine and Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics position
stands recommend ([PF-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)):

| Exercise duration | Carbohydrate | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 45 min | Not needed | Mouth rinse may help at maximal intensity |
| 45–75 min | Small amounts | Mouth rinse or a few sips |
| 1–2.5 h | 30–60 g/h | Any format |
| Over 2.5–3 h | Up to 90 g/h | Glucose–fructose mixtures absorb faster |

Carbohydrate concentration matters. A 6–8% solution empties from the stomach reasonably
quickly and delivers meaningful carbohydrate; above about 8%, gastric emptying slows and
gastrointestinal distress becomes more likely
([PF-07](generated/evidence-tables.md)). This is why a 16 g stick with 12 g sugar in
473 mL (2.5%) is a hydration product, not a fueling product, while a 6% sports drink can
do both.

## Sodium: what it does and does not do

**Does:** increase thirst and voluntary fluid intake, improve palatability of water,
improve fluid retention when intake matches losses, and attenuate the fall in plasma
sodium during prolonged exercise ([HY-07](generated/evidence-tables.md),
[HY-04](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

**Does not:** improve performance when fluid intake is matched
([PF-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)), prevent cramps
([CR-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)), or prevent hyponatraemia if the athlete
overdrinks ([SC-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

The 2025 review of sodium intake for athletes puts it precisely: sodium intake during
exercise does not appear to influence performance unless it also produces more optimal
fluid intake. Replacement should complement fluid balance, not be treated as an
independent strategy.

Sodium concentration also affects tolerability. Solutions above roughly 333 mg/100 mL
(145 mmol/L) can be unpalatable and draw water into the gut, causing bloating. A
practical compromise is 100–120 mg/100 mL for voluntary drinking, or a more concentrated
stick taken in smaller volumes across an hour.

## Heat: the multiplier

Heat adds three problems: higher sweat losses, higher cardiovascular strain for the same
power output, and higher core temperature. Acclimation addresses all three.

**Heat acclimation** (7–14 days of progressive heat exposure) lowers core temperature and
heart rate at a given workload, expands plasma volume, raises sweat rate, and lowers sweat
sodium by roughly 9–23 mmol/L ([PF-08](generated/evidence-tables.md),
[HT-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). It is the single most effective intervention
available to athletes competing in heat, and it is free.

**Cooling**: ice slushies, cold towels, cold water immersion, improves performance and
reduces thermal strain in hot conditions. It is more effective than any electrolyte
product.

**Salt tablets** do not prevent heat illness
([HT-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Heat illness is a cooling failure. Exertional
heat stroke requires immediate cold-water immersion; oral fluids are adjunctive and can
be dangerous if the athlete is obtunded ([HT-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

## Pre-exercise hyperhydration

Sodium loading before exercise can expand plasma volume and improve performance in heat
in some trials, particularly cycling ([PF-06](generated/evidence-tables.md)). The
protocols use 10–20 mL/kg of fluid with enough sodium to reach roughly 275–420 mg/100 mL,
consumed 2–4 hours before exercise.

It is not routine. It helps when large losses are expected, when drinking during exercise
is difficult, and when the athlete tolerates the fluid load. It can cause gastrointestinal
distress, and the evidence in running is weaker than in cycling.

## What to do with this

| Situation | Priority |
| --- | --- |
| Under 60 min, moderate conditions | Water; no product needed |
| 1–2.5 h, moderate to hot | Fluid + 30–60 g/h carbohydrate; 300–600 mg/h sodium |
| Over 2.5 h | Fluid + 60–90 g/h carbohydrate; 300–700 mg/h sodium |
| Hot event, heavy sweater | Acclimatise; 700–1,200 mg/h sodium; split doses |
| Hot event, light sweater | Fluid and carbohydrate first; sodium secondary |
| Weight-making or cool-weather race | Do not overdrink; sodium is optional |

The recurring theme: **electrolytes are a supporting actor**. Fluid, carbohydrate,
acclimation and pacing are the leads.
