# Sweat, sodium loss and fluid balance

## How sweat is made

The eccrine sweat gland coils in the dermis and secretes a precursor fluid that is
roughly isotonic with plasma. As that fluid travels up the duct, sodium and chloride are
reabsorbed and returned to the body. What reaches the skin is therefore **hypotonic**:
water with some salt, not a filtrate of plasma.

Two consequences follow. First, sweating costs the body more water than salt, which is
why thirst is a water signal. Second, the amount of salt reabsorbed is adjustable, and
that adjustment is the main reason sweat sodium varies so much between people and across
seasons.

## How much you sweat

Whole-body sweating rate typically ranges from **0.5 to 2.0 L/h**, with values above
3 L/h possible in extreme heat, high intensity or large athletes
([PH-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Sweat rate is driven by metabolic heat
production, environment, clothing and body size, and it rises with heat acclimation and
aerobic fitness.

A useful planning figure: a 70 kg runner at moderate intensity in temperate conditions
often loses 1.0–1.5 L/h. In hot, humid conditions the same runner can lose 2 L/h or more,
because sweat that drips off does not evaporate and cool.

## What is in sweat

| Component | Typical concentration | Per litre |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium | 10–90 mmol/L (mean ~42) | 230–2,070 mg (mean ~966 mg) |
| Chloride | 20–60 mmol/L | 710–2,130 mg |
| Potassium | 4–8 mmol/L | 156–313 mg |
| Magnesium | under 1 mmol/L | under 24 mg |
| Calcium | under 1 mmol/L | under 40 mg |
| Lactate, urea, ammonia | small | n/a |

Sweat sodium is the variable that matters. A 6-fold spread between individuals is normal,
and the same person can differ by 30–50% between sessions depending on intensity, heat
acclimation and diet ([PH-01](generated/evidence-tables.md),
[PH-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

At the population mean, one litre of sweat contains about **966 mg sodium**, or 2.5 g of
salt. A heavy, salty sweater at 70 mmol/L loses about 1,610 mg sodium per litre; a
light sweater at 20 mmol/L loses about 460 mg. This is why "1,000 mg per hour" is a
reasonable average and a poor universal prescription.

## What changes sweat sodium

**Heat acclimation.** Repeated heat exposure lowers sweat sodium by roughly 9–23 mmol/L
at a matched sweat rate, typically about 15 mmol/L, with the effect appearing within days
and plateauing over one to two weeks ([PH-06](generated/evidence-tables.md),
[HT-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). The mechanism is increased aldosterone
sensitivity and greater sodium reabsorption in the sweat duct. Acclimation also raises
sweat rate, so total sodium loss may not fall as much as concentration does.

**Dietary sodium.** A high-sodium diet suppresses aldosterone and raises sweat sodium; a
low-sodium diet does the reverse ([PH-07](generated/evidence-tables.md)). This is
adaptation, not pathology, the body is defending sodium balance.

**Intensity and duration.** Higher sweat rates deliver more precursor fluid than the duct
can reabsorb, so concentration rises with intensity in many people.

**Site and method.** A patch on the forearm does not measure whole-body sweat. Regional
sweat sodium can differ by 20–40% from the whole-body value, and patch techniques vary in
collection time, skin preparation and analysis.

**Sex and body size.** Women tend to have lower sweat sodium than men in several datasets,
partly through body size and sweat rate rather than sex itself.

**Genetics.** Twin and family studies show a substantial heritable component to sweat
sodium, which is why some athletes are "salty" regardless of training.

## How to estimate your own losses

The two variables are independent: how much you sweat, and how salty it is. You can
measure the first precisely and the second only approximately.

**Sweat rate (accurate enough to act on).**

1. Weigh yourself nude before a representative session.
2. Record everything you drink (weigh bottles before and after).
3. Weigh yourself nude afterwards, towel-dried.
4. Sweat loss (L) = (pre-weight − post-weight) + fluid consumed − urine passed.
5. Divide by hours.

A 1 kg change is approximately 1 L. Repeat in the conditions you actually train in; a
cool-weather session tells you little about a hot race.

**Sweat sodium (approximate).**

Patch-based sweat tests cost money and carry real measurement error. They are useful for
answering "am I a very salty sweater?" and poor for prescribing a precise milligram
target. A reasonable interpretation:

| Sweat sodium | Interpretation | Practical sodium target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 30 mmol/L | Light sweater | 300–500 mg/h |
| 30–50 mmol/L | Typical | 500–800 mg/h |
| 50–70 mmol/L | Salty sweater | 800–1,100 mg/h |
| Over 70 mmol/L | Very salty, usually visible salt stains | 1,000–1,500 mg/h, split into doses |

These are starting points. The real test is whether fluid intake, thirst, stomach comfort
and performance hold up in training.

## Fluid balance: the narrow band that matters

Hydration is a balance, not a target volume.

- **Losing 2% of body mass** is where endurance performance commonly starts to fall, and
  cardiovascular and thermal strain rise ([HY-01](generated/evidence-tables.md),
  [HY-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)).
- **Losing more than 4–5%** progressively impairs performance and, in heat, raises the risk
  of heat illness.
- **Gaining weight during exercise** is the signature of overdrinking and the strongest
  modifiable risk factor for exercise-associated hyponatraemia
  ([HY-08](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

The practical target during exercise is therefore to replace **most, not all** of the
deficit: aim to finish within about 2% of starting weight. Drinking to thirst does this
well for most people in most conditions. Planned drinking helps when thirst is blunted
(cold, high intensity, very long events) or when losses are extreme.

## Sodium and water are one decision

Sodium without water does not hydrate; water without sodium is retained poorly. The
combination determines plasma volume. Post-exercise, replacing 100% of the fluid deficit
with plain water still leaves a deficit, because some of the water is excreted as urine.
Replacing 125–150% of the deficit with a sodium-containing drink restores net balance
more reliably ([RC-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

This is the strongest evidence-based case for electrolyte products: not that athletes are
sodium-deficient, but that sodium improves the retention of the fluid you drink.

## What "dehydration" feels like, and why it misleads

Thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, headache and fatigue are real signals but non-specific.
Dark urine can reflect B vitamins; thirst can be blunted in cold or at high intensity;
and in overhydration, symptoms overlap with dehydration. Body mass change is the only
field measure that distinguishes the two reliably.

That is why the practical advice is boring and correct: **weigh in and out, drink to
thirst, include sodium when sweat losses are large, and do not try to "win" hydration.**
