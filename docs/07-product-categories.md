# Product categories compared

The shelf is organised by format, but format is a poor guide to what a product does. A
tablet and a stick can both deliver 300 mg sodium; a "sports drink" and an "oral
rehydration solution" can differ three-fold in sodium. What matters is **composition per
serving**, which is why every product in this review is scored on the same fields.

## The seven formats

### 1. Electrolyte powders (sticks and tubs)

The dominant direct-to-consumer format. A stick is a fixed dose in a sachet; a tub is a
scoop the user measures. Advantages: high sodium per serving is easy, formulation is
flexible, and the user controls the water volume. Disadvantages: cost per serving is
usually the highest of any format, and the scoop is a source of dosing error.

In this database, powders range from 400 to 1,120 mg sodium per serving (excluding ORS)
and from $0.93 to $3.50 per 1,000 mg sodium. A 6 g stick can hold 1,000 mg sodium because
sodium chloride is only 39% sodium by mass; reaching 1,000 mg requires about 2.5 g of
salt, which is most of the stick.

### 2. Tablets

Effervescent tablets are the cheapest way to make water taste like something and the
weakest way to replace sodium. A typical tablet delivers 300 mg sodium, enough for about
300 mL of average sweat. Cost per 1,000 mg sodium is usually $2.50–$4.00 because the
tablet also carries acid, bicarbonate, binder and flavour.

Tablets are reasonable for:

- flavour and mild electrolyte replacement in moderate conditions,
- people who dislike sweet drinks,
- travel and storage (no powder to spill).

They are a poor choice for a heavy sweater in heat, who would need three or four tablets
per hour.

### 3. Ready-to-drink sports drinks

The mass-market baseline: 270–400 mg sodium per 591 mL bottle and 25–36 g carbohydrate.
They are formulated to be palatable, not to match sweat. At 457 mg/L (20 mmol/L), Gatorade
delivers about half the sodium concentration of average sweat; Powerade at 677 mg/L
delivers about two-thirds. Both are hypotonic to slightly hypertonic depending on the
product.

The advantage is availability and price per bottle. The disadvantage is cost per
milligram of sodium: $6.23–$8.81 per 1,000 mg, the worst value in the database, because
you are paying for water, packaging and sugar.

Sugar-free sports drinks keep the sodium and remove the carbohydrate. They are fine for
hydration and useless for fueling.

### 4. Oral rehydration solutions

ORS is a medicine, not a lifestyle product. The WHO reduced-osmolarity formulation is
precise: **75 mmol/L sodium, 20 mmol/L potassium, 65 mmol/L chloride, 10 mmol/L citrate,
75 mmol/L glucose, 245 mOsm/L**. The glucose is not there for calories; it drives
sodium-glucose cotransport, which is how water is absorbed most efficiently.

In this database, ORS products are the cheapest source of sodium by a wide margin
($0.23–$1.57 per 1,000 mg) and the highest sodium per serving (851–1,725 mg). They are
also the least palatable to many people, and the most likely to cause bloating if drunk
in large volumes quickly.

ORS is the right choice for diarrhoea and for severe sweat losses where cost and sodium
density matter. It is not a sports drink, and sports drinks are not ORS.

### 5. Electrolyte capsules

Capsules deliver sodium without water. That is useful when you cannot carry fluid, and
problematic when you take them without enough water, the salt load draws water into the
gut and can cause nausea. SaltStick delivers 215 mg sodium per capsule with 349 mg
chloride; Hammer Endurolytes delivers only 80 mg sodium per two-capsule serving.

Capsules are best used as a supplement to a drinking plan, not as a substitute for one.
Cost per 1,000 mg sodium ranges from $1.16 (SaltStick) to $5.20 (Hammer).

### 6. Electrolyte drinks (RTD, premium)

Bottled premium electrolyte drinks typically deliver 500–700 mg sodium per 473 mL and
cost $3–$4. They are convenient and well formulated but expensive per milligram:
$4.76 per 1,000 mg sodium for BIOLYTE in this sample.

### 7. Enhanced waters

This is where the word "electrolyte" does the most work and means the least. Two products
in the database illustrate the range:

- **CORE Hydration** declares 0 mg sodium and lists potassium bicarbonate, magnesium
  chloride and calcium chloride as ingredients with no amounts. It is flavoured water with
  trace minerals.
- **PRIME Hydration** declares 10 mg sodium and 700 mg potassium per bottle, a
  potassium-forward drink with essentially no sodium.
- **BODYARMOR** declares 30 mg sodium and 680 mg potassium per 16 oz, the same pattern.

These are not frauds; they are different products. But an athlete who drinks one believing
they have replaced sweat sodium has replaced about 3% of it. The potassium dose is
pharmacologically interesting and irrelevant to sweat replacement.

## Format is not composition

The same category can span a 40-fold range in sodium:

| Format | Sodium per serving in this database | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ORS | 490–1,725 mg | Diarrhoea, severe losses |
| Electrolyte powder | 400–1,120 mg | Planned exercise, heat |
| Electrolyte drink (RTD) | 700 mg | Convenience |
| Sports drink | 270–400 mg | Mass-market hydration + fuel |
| Electrolyte capsule | 80–215 mg | Sodium without fluid |
| Electrolyte tablet | 300 mg | Light replacement, flavour |
| Enhanced water | 0–30 mg | Everyday flavouring |

## Osmolarity and tolerability

Osmolarity determines how a drink behaves in the gut. Hypotonic fluids (<270 mOsm/L) are
absorbed fastest and hydrate best; isotonic (~270–330) are close to plasma; hypertonic
(>330) draw water into the gut and can cause bloating and diarrhoea.

Approximate values:

- WHO ORS: 245 mOsm/L (hypotonic, despite high sodium, because glucose is controlled).
- Gatorade-class drink: ~300 mOsm/L (isotonic).
- Precision PH 1000: stated 161 mmol/kg (~163 mOsm/L, hypotonic).
- A 1,000 mg sodium stick in 500 mL: roughly 2,000 mg/L sodium plus potassium and
  magnesium salts, hypertonic if drunk at that concentration, hypotonic if spread over
  1 L.

The practical rule: **if a stick makes you bloated, add water**. The sodium dose stays the
same; the concentration falls.

## Serving-size games

Three label practices distort comparisons:

1. **Per-stick versus per-bottle.** A 1 L bottle may contain 2.8 servings, so the sodium
   per bottle is nearly three times the number on the panel.
2. **Two-capsule servings.** Hammer's 80 mg sodium is per two capsules, not per capsule.
3. **Scoop weight drift.** A tub's scoop can be 6.2 g in one flavour and 7 g in another,
   changing the sodium per serving by 13%.

The database normalises all of these to sodium per stated serving and cost per 1,000 mg.

## Which format for which job

| Job | Best format | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diarrhoea | ORS sachet | Exact WHO formula, cheapest sodium |
| Long endurance event | Powder or sports drink | Flexible concentration, carbohydrate |
| Hot workout under 90 min | Powder or tablet | Sodium without excess sugar |
| Gym session under 60 min | Water | Nothing to replace |
| Can't carry fluid | Capsule + water | Dose without volume |
| Everyday flavouring | Tablet or enhanced water | Low dose is fine |
| Post-exercise rehydration | Powder, ORS or milk + food | Sodium and fluid together |
