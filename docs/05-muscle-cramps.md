# Muscle cramps: the electrolyte story that does not hold up

Exercise-associated muscle cramps (EAMC) are painful, involuntary contractions that
usually affect the calves, hamstrings or quadriceps during or shortly after exercise.
They are common, memorable and almost universally blamed on "electrolyte imbalance." The
evidence does not support that explanation as the main mechanism.

## The two competing explanations

**The dehydration/electrolyte hypothesis.** Sweating loses water and sodium; if they are
not replaced, extracellular fluid volume and sodium concentration fall; neuromuscular
irritability rises; cramps follow. This story is intuitive, widely repeated, and
consistent with some clinical case reports.

**The neuromuscular hypothesis.** Cramps result from altered spinal reflex control:
muscle spindle and Golgi tendon organ afferents become hyperexcitable under fatigue,
causing sustained motor neuron firing. This explains why cramps occur in fatigued muscle,
why they respond to stretching, and why they happen in cool conditions and in swimmers who
are not sweating heavily.

## What the evidence shows

Schwellnus reviewed the field and concluded that support for the electrolyte and
dehydration hypotheses came mainly from anecdotal reports and small case series, while
**four prospective cohort studies did not support them**
([CR-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). The neuromuscular hypothesis, by contrast, has
human experimental, epidemiological and animal support.

The largest field test of the sodium hypothesis found no relationship between total sodium
intake and cramping in ultramarathon runners over races lasting up to 30 hours
([CR-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Cramping, nausea, hyponatraemia and dehydration
were all unrelated to sodium intake. Runners who took more sodium actually lost less
weight, but that did not translate into fewer cramps.

Cramp-prone athletes do tend to lose more sodium in sweat in some studies
([CR-06](generated/evidence-tables.md)), but those studies are observational and
confounded by sweat rate, body size, fitness and training. A salty sweater is not
necessarily a cramper, and a cramper is not necessarily salty.

## Does anything in a bottle help?

**Sodium.** No trial shows sodium prevents EAMC. There is a plausible edge case: if an
athlete becomes dehydrated and then drinks a large volume of plain water, plasma sodium
falls quickly, and that dilution can increase cramp susceptibility. An oral rehydration
solution prevented that fall and increased cramp threshold in a small laboratory study
([CR-05](generated/evidence-tables.md)). That is a real finding, but it is about
**avoiding dilution**, not about sodium deficiency.

**Pickle juice.** A small crossover trial found that pickle juice relieved electrically
induced cramps faster than water, about 85 seconds versus 134
([CR-04](generated/evidence-tables.md)). The effect is too fast to be absorption, and is
attributed to a trigeminal reflex from the vinegar. Mustard and other sharp tastes may do
the same. The evidence is small and the outcome is a surrogate, but the mechanism is
plausible and the risk is low.

**Magnesium.** Cochrane found no clinically meaningful cramp prophylaxis in older adults,
and no randomised trials in exercise-associated cramps
([CR-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). There is no pharmacological reason to expect an
acute effect.

**Potassium.** No evidence. Sweat potassium losses are small, and serum potassium is
tightly regulated.

**Carbohydrate.** Indirectly relevant: carbohydrate delays fatigue, and fatigue is the
strongest cramp trigger. That is a fueling effect, not an electrolyte effect.

**Stretching and load management.** Stretching the cramping muscle is the standard acute
treatment. Reducing fatigue, building strength and progressing load appear to reduce
recurrence, though the evidence is observational
([CR-07](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

## Why the myth persists

Cramps are episodic, unpredictable and memorable. A cramper who takes salt and does not
cramp next time concludes that salt worked; a cramper who takes salt and cramps anyway
concludes that they took too little. There is no placebo-controlled feedback loop in
ordinary training. Marketing fills the gap with a simple story and a product.

## Practical approach

1. **Treat the acute cramp**: stop, stretch the muscle, massage it, and drink fluid if you
   are also dehydrated.
2. **Look for the cause**: sudden increases in volume or intensity, insufficient
   conditioning, heat, fatigue, and prior cramp history are the consistent risk factors.
3. **Fix the training**: progressive loading, strength work and pacing do more than any
   supplement.
4. **Use sodium for hydration, not as cramp insurance**: if you are a heavy sweater in
   heat, replacing some sodium is reasonable for fluid balance. Do not expect it to stop
   cramps.
5. **Do not use quinine** for nocturnal cramps; it is no longer recommended because of
   thrombocytopenia and arrhythmia risk.

## The honest summary

EAMC is a neuromuscular fatigue problem with an electrolyte story bolted on. Sodium may
matter at the margins, particularly when large volumes of plain water dilute plasma
sodium, but the evidence does not support electrolyte products as a cramp-prevention
strategy. Anyone selling "cramp relief" on the basis of sodium or magnesium is selling a
mechanism, not a demonstrated effect.
