# Dehydration, overhydration and electrolyte imbalances

## Dehydration

Dehydration is a body-water deficit, usually with a sodium deficit proportional to sweat
losses. It is classified by percentage of body mass lost:

| Deficit | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| 1% | Thirst, impaired thermoregulation begins |
| 2% | Endurance performance starts to fall; cardiovascular strain rises |
| 3–4% | Clear performance impairment, higher core temperature, higher heart rate |
| 5–6% | Severe impairment, heat exhaustion risk |
| Over 7% | Medical emergency |

**Signs:** thirst, dry mouth, dark and concentrated urine, headache, fatigue, dizziness on
standing, reduced sweat rate late in exercise, and in severe cases confusion and
collapse.

**Treatment:** oral fluid with sodium if the person can drink; intravenous fluid if they
cannot or if circulation is compromised. Rehydrate over hours, not minutes, and replace
125–150% of the estimated deficit ([RC-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

## Overhydration and exercise-associated hyponatraemia

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia (EAH) is a serum sodium below 135 mmol/L during or
after exercise. It is the most dangerous hydration problem in endurance sport because it
is caused by the behaviour athletes are told to adopt.

### How common is it?

In the 2002 Boston Marathon, **13% of 488 finishers** had hyponatraemia and **0.6% had
critical hyponatraemia** (serum sodium ≤120 mmol/L) ([R05](../docs/references.md)). Across
marathon studies the reported range is roughly 0–22%, with a mean around 8%
([SC-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Symptomatic cases are much rarer; deaths are rarer
still, but they happen in marathons, ultramarathons, triathlons, hiking, military training,
police training, American football and fraternity hazing.

### What causes it?

The primary mechanism is **drinking more hypotonic fluid than the kidneys can excrete**,
often combined with non-osmotic stimulation of vasopressin (ADH), which prevents free-water
excretion ([SC-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Sodium loss in sweat contributes in
some cases, but it is usually secondary. The 2015 consensus concluded that sodium ingestion
may attenuate the fall in blood sodium but **cannot prevent EAH when fluid intake is
excessive**.

### Risk factors

- Weight gain during exercise (the strongest modifiable factor; OR 4.2 in Boston).
- Fluid intake over about 3 L during a race.
- Exercise lasting more than 4 hours.
- Slow pace.
- Low body mass index (small athletes following advice designed for larger people).
- High or low BMI in different studies.
- Event inexperience.
- NSAID use (association, not proven causation).
- Extreme hot or cold conditions.
- Ready availability of fluids.

### Symptoms

Mild: bloating, nausea, headache, light-headedness.
Severe: vomiting, confusion, altered mental status, seizures, coma, respiratory distress.
Symptoms reflect cerebral oedema and can progress rapidly.

### Prevention

1. **Do not gain weight during exercise.** Weigh in and out.
2. **Drink to thirst** rather than to a fixed schedule
   ([HY-08](generated/evidence-tables.md)).
3. **Replace most, not all, of the deficit.** Finishing 1–2% down is normal and safe.
4. **Use sodium when sweat losses are large**, but do not treat it as protection against
   overdrinking.
5. **Educate athletes and race staff** that more fluid is not safer.

### Treatment

- **Asymptomatic or mild:** restrict fluids until urination resumes; oral hypertonic
  solution (concentrated bouillon, or 3% saline with flavouring) may be used.
- **Severe (neurological symptoms):** **100 mL bolus of 3% NaCl intravenously**, repeated
  up to twice at 10-minute intervals, aiming to raise serum sodium by 4–5 mmol/L or until
  symptoms resolve ([S32](generated/standards.md), [S33](generated/standards.md)).
- **Do not** give hypotonic or isotonic intravenous fluid as the primary treatment; it can
  worsen cerebral oedema.
- Transfer to hospital for monitoring and definitive care.

This is a medical emergency. The treatment protocol belongs to clinicians, not to a
hydration product.

## Hypernatraemia

Serum sodium above 145 mmol/L, usually from water deficit rather than salt excess. Causes
include inadequate intake, high sweat losses without replacement, diarrhoea, vomiting, and
osmotic diuresis. Salt tablets without water can cause it, but that is rare.

Symptoms: intense thirst, dry mucous membranes, confusion, seizures, coma. Treatment is
gradual rehydration; correct slowly to avoid cerebral oedema.

## Water intoxication

Drinking several litres of water in a short period can overwhelm the kidney's excretion
capacity (about 0.8–1.0 L/h) and cause acute hyponatraemia. Cases occur in endurance
events, military training, and water-drinking contests. It is the same mechanism as EAH,
without the exercise.

## Heat illness

Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache, core temperature
38–40°C. Treat by moving to shade, cooling, and oral fluids if alert.

Exertional heat stroke: core temperature above 40°C with central nervous system
dysfunction (confusion, agitation, seizure, coma). This is a life-threatening emergency.
**Cold-water immersion is the definitive treatment**; cooling must begin immediately
([HT-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Fluids are adjunctive. Do not delay cooling to
give a drink.

## Electrolyte imbalances at a glance

| Imbalance | Typical cause | Symptoms | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hyponatraemia | Overdrinking, sweat sodium loss, diuretics | Headache, nausea, confusion, seizures | Restrict fluid; hypertonic saline if severe |
| Hypernatraemia | Water deficit, salt without water | Thirst, confusion, seizures | Gradual rehydration |
| Hypokalaemia | Diuretics, vomiting, diarrhoea | Weakness, cramps, arrhythmia | Replace potassium, treat cause |
| Hyperkalaemia | CKD, ACE inhibitors, potassium salts | Weakness, palpitations, arrhythmia | Emergency; stop potassium, treat |
| Hypomagnesaemia | Diuretics, PPIs, alcohol | Cramps, tremor, arrhythmia | Replace magnesium |
| Hypocalcaemia | Low intake, vitamin D deficiency, hypoparathyroidism | Tetany, cramps, seizures | Replace calcium and vitamin D |

## When to seek medical care

- Confusion, seizure, collapse, or reduced consciousness during or after exercise.
- Inability to keep fluids down.
- No urine for 8–12 hours.
- Weight gain during an endurance event with headache or nausea.
- Swelling of hands or face during a long event.
- Palpitations or severe muscle weakness.

The common thread: **hydration problems are diagnosed by body weight and blood sodium, not
by how thirsty you feel.** Anyone who gains weight during an endurance event and feels
unwell should be assessed, not given more fluid.
