# Recovery: what electrolytes do and do not fix

"Recovery" is the most heavily marketed and least regulated word in the hydration aisle.
A recovery drink can mean anything from a complete carbohydrate-protein meal replacement
to flavoured water with a vitamin blend. The physiology is narrower than the marketing.

## The four things recovery actually requires

1. **Fluid** to restore plasma volume.
2. **Sodium** to retain that fluid and restore extracellular volume.
3. **Carbohydrate** to restore muscle and liver glycogen.
4. **Protein** to support muscle protein synthesis, if the next training session is soon
   or the athlete is in energy deficit.

Electrolytes contribute to items 1 and 2. They do nothing meaningful for 3 and 4.

## Rehydration after exercise

Sweat losses are hypotonic, so replacing them with plain water restores volume but also
stimulates urine output. The classic finding is that **125–150% of the fluid deficit**
must be consumed, with sodium, to restore net balance
([RC-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). For every kilogram lost, drink about 1.25–1.5 L
over the next few hours.

Sodium-containing drinks reduce urine loss compared with water
([RC-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)), and the beverage hydration index ranks oral
rehydration solution and milk above water for retention
([HY-05](generated/evidence-tables.md)). A normal meal with salt does the same job and
brings carbohydrate and protein with it.

Practical post-exercise protocol:

- Weigh yourself before and after.
- Replace 125–150% of the loss over 2–4 hours, not in one sitting.
- Include 30–50 mmol/L sodium (roughly 700–1,150 mg/L) or eat salted food.
- Add carbohydrate if the next session is within 24 hours.
- Add 20–30 g protein if the goal is adaptation rather than pure rehydration.

## Glycogen: electrolytes are irrelevant

Muscle glycogen resynthesis depends on carbohydrate availability and total energy, not on
sodium, potassium or magnesium ([RC-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). The ACSM position
stand recommends 1.0–1.2 g/kg/hour of carbohydrate in the first few hours after exercise
when rapid refuelling matters, with protein adding a modest benefit.

A recovery drink that provides 20 g carbohydrate and 500 mg sodium is a hydration product
with a small snack attached. It is not a refuelling strategy.

## Muscle soreness and damage

There is no credible evidence that electrolyte drinks reduce delayed-onset muscle
soreness, markers of muscle damage, or inflammation. Claims to that effect usually rest on
one of three things:

- **Confusing hydration with recovery.** Being dehydrated makes you feel worse; rehydrating
  makes you feel better. That is not an effect on muscle damage.
- **Adding protein or carbohydrate**, which may have small effects on soreness and
  perceived recovery, and then attributing them to electrolytes.
- **Adding anti-inflammatory or analgesic ingredients**, which are drugs, not electrolytes.

## Alcohol and hangovers

Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, causing diuresis, and can cause gastrointestinal losses.
Fluid and electrolytes may ease some symptoms. There is no controlled evidence that an
electrolyte product speeds alcohol metabolism, prevents a hangover, or protects the liver
([C17](generated/claims-table.md)). The most effective intervention is time and not
drinking to excess.

## What recovery products get right

- **Sodium plus fluid** genuinely improves retention after a sweaty session.
- **Carbohydrate plus sodium** improves palatability and voluntary intake, which matters
  when athletes do not feel like drinking.
- **Convenience** is real. A stick in a bottle is easier than making a meal after a
  race.

## What they oversell

- "Replenishes all five electrolytes": magnesium and calcium losses are trivial and
  dietary intake dominates.
- "Speeds muscle recovery": no evidence beyond rehydration and nutrition.
- "Prevents cramps": see [the cramp chapter](05-muscle-cramps.md).
- "Detox" or "cellular recovery": meaningless.

## Bottom line

For recovery, the hierarchy is **fluid, sodium, carbohydrate, protein, sleep**. An
electrolyte product can deliver the first two conveniently. It cannot substitute for the
last three.
