# Dosage guidance

This chapter turns the evidence into practical numbers. It is written for healthy adults
with normal kidney function. Kidney disease, heart failure, pregnancy, hypertension and
medication use change the answer, sometimes completely.

## Start with the decision, not the dose

```
Do you sweat heavily or exercise long/hot?
├── No  → water and food. No product needed.
└── Yes → Are you exercising longer than ~60–90 min, or in heat?
          ├── No  → water; optional low-sodium tablet for taste.
          └── Yes → Do you need carbohydrate?
                    ├── Yes → carbohydrate-electrolyte drink at 4–9% CHO.
                    └── No  → electrolyte drink at 500–1,000 mg/L sodium.
```

## The three numbers to set

**1. Fluid.** Measure it, do not guess. Weigh in and out of a representative session:

`loss (L) = pre-weight − post-weight + fluid drunk − urine`

Aim to replace 60–80% of that during exercise and the rest afterwards. Finishing within 2%
of starting weight is the target.

**2. Sodium.** Estimate from sweat volume and concentration:

`sodium loss (mg) = sweat volume (L) × sodium concentration (mmol/L) × 23`

A typical athlete at 1.2 L/h and 40 mmol/L loses about 1,100 mg/h. A salty sweater at
1.5 L/h and 65 mmol/L loses about 2,240 mg/h. Most people do not need to replace all of it
during exercise; 40–70% is a reasonable target.

**3. Carbohydrate.** Follow duration:

| Duration | Carbohydrate |
| --- | --- |
| Under 45 min | 0 |
| 45–75 min | Small amounts or mouth rinse |
| 1–2.5 h | 30–60 g/h |
| Over 2.5 h | 60–90 g/h, multiple transportable |

## Sodium targets by scenario

| Scenario | Sodium per hour | Fluid per hour | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Everyday, no exercise | 0 added | Thirst | Food covers 2–4 g/day |
| Under 60 min moderate | 0 | 400–800 mL | Water |
| 1–2.5 h moderate/hot | 300–600 mg | 500–1,000 mL | 20–30 mmol/L drink |
| Over 2.5 h | 300–700 mg | 500–1,000 mL | Plus carbohydrate |
| Heavy sweater in heat | 700–1,200 mg | 1,000–1,500 mL | Split doses |
| Post-exercise rehydration | 500–1,000 mg total | 125–150% of deficit | With a meal |
| Diarrhoea (adult) | WHO ORS | 200–400 mL per stool | Medical product |
| Cramp-prone | 300–600 mg | Match losses | Fix fatigue first |

These ranges are deliberately wide. The correct dose is the one that keeps you within 2%
of starting weight without gastrointestinal distress.

## Timing

**Before exercise.** Drink 5–10 mL/kg in the 2–4 hours before. If you are heat-sensitive
or expect large losses, add sodium at 275–420 mg/100 mL to improve retention. Do not
overload; a full stomach is a liability.

**During.** Start drinking early, then match losses. Small, frequent volumes are better
tolerated than large boluses. If using a concentrated stick, spread it across the hour
rather than drinking it in one go.

**After.** Replace 125–150% of the deficit over 2–4 hours with sodium-containing fluid and
a meal. Add carbohydrate and protein if the next session is soon.

## What not to do

- **Do not take salt tablets without water.** They draw water into the gut and can cause
  nausea or hypernatraemia.
- **Do not chase a fixed hourly sodium number** from a sweat test. Use it as a starting
  point and adjust to tolerance.
- **Do not drink to a schedule that makes you gain weight.** Weight gain during exercise is
  the main risk factor for hyponatraemia.
- **Do not use potassium-heavy drinks if you have kidney disease, heart failure, or take
  ACE inhibitors, ARBs, MRAs or potassium-sparing diuretics.**
- **Do not use sports drinks for diarrhoea.** Use ORS.
- **Do not assume "electrolyte" means sodium.** Read the milligrams.

## Special populations

**Children.** Children have higher surface-area-to-mass ratios and different sweat rates.
For exercise, water and normal food are usually enough; for diarrhoea, use commercial ORS
and follow clinical guidance. Do not give adult high-sodium sticks to children.

**Pregnancy.** Fluid needs rise modestly. High-sodium products are not appropriate for
pregnancy-induced hypertension or pre-eclampsia. Ask a clinician.

**Older adults.** Thirst is blunted and kidney concentrating ability declines. A planned
fluid schedule may be more useful than thirst, but overdrinking remains a risk.

**People with hypertension.** Population sodium reduction lowers blood pressure. Do not add
sodium supplements without a clinical reason; if you exercise heavily in heat, discuss a
plan with your clinician.

**People with CKD or heart failure.** Sodium and potassium handling is impaired. Fluid and
electrolyte plans should be set by the treating team, not by a product label.

## A simple default plan

For a healthy adult doing a 2-hour run or ride in warm weather:

- 500–750 mL/h of fluid.
- 500–800 mg/h sodium (one 1,000 mg stick spread over 90 minutes, or a 20–30 mmol/L
  sports drink).
- 30–60 g/h carbohydrate if the session is hard or over 90 minutes.
- Weigh before and after; adjust next time.

That is the whole protocol. Everything else is optimisation at the margins.
