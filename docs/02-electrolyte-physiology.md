# What the five electrolytes do

An electrolyte is a mineral that carries a charge when dissolved. The body uses that
charge to move water, generate nerve signals, contract muscle and keep pH in range. The
five in sports products are sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium and chloride. They are
not interchangeable, and the amounts that matter differ by orders of magnitude.

## Sodium (Na⁺)

**Where it is.** About 90% of body sodium sits outside cells, in plasma and interstitial
fluid. It is the main determinant of extracellular fluid volume and plasma osmolality.

**What it does.**

- **Sets osmolality.** Water follows sodium. Change plasma sodium and you change where
  water sits, which is why sodium is the effective solute in rehydration.
- **Maintains blood volume and blood pressure.** Sodium retention expands extracellular
  volume; renal sodium handling is the final common path for most blood-pressure drugs.
- **Drives nerve and muscle excitability.** The sodium gradient across the membrane is
  the basis of the action potential.
- **Powers intestinal absorption.** Sodium-glucose cotransport (SGLT1) pulls sodium,
  glucose and water into the gut together; that is the mechanism behind oral rehydration
  therapy.

**Sweat loss.** Sweat sodium averages about 42 mmol/L across 1,944 tests, with a range of
17–106 mmol/L ([evidence PH-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). At 40 mmol/L, one litre
of sweat contains about 920 mg sodium, roughly 2.3 g of salt.

**Requirement.** The adequate intake is 1,500 mg/day and the chronic disease risk
reduction intake is 2,300 mg/day for adults; WHO recommends under 2,000 mg/day
([standards](generated/standards.md)). Typical US intake is 3,400–3,600 mg/day, so most
people are not short of sodium.

## Potassium (K⁺)

**Where it is.** About 98% of body potassium is inside cells. Serum potassium is therefore
a poor measure of total body stores.

**What it does.**

- **Sets the resting membrane potential.** The potassium gradient across the membrane
  determines how excitable a nerve or muscle cell is. Small changes in serum potassium
  can cause arrhythmias.
- **Counterbalances sodium.** Higher potassium intake promotes sodium excretion, lowers
  blood pressure and reduces stroke risk.
- **Supports acid-base balance and cell volume.**

**Sweat loss.** Small: about 6 mmol/L (range 3–8) in marathoners
([PH-04](generated/evidence-tables.md)). A litre of sweat contains roughly 230 mg
potassium, about a quarter of a banana.

**Requirement.** 3,400 mg/day for men and 2,600 mg/day for women (adequate intake); WHO
suggests at least 3,510 mg/day from food. There is no tolerable upper intake level for
healthy adults, because healthy kidneys excrete excess. That changes completely in
chronic kidney disease and with drugs that impair potassium excretion.

## Magnesium (Mg²⁺)

**Where it is.** Mostly in bone and inside cells; about 1% is extracellular.

**What it does.**

- **Cofactor for more than 300 enzymes**, including every reaction that uses ATP.
- **Muscle relaxation and neuromuscular transmission.** It gates calcium channels and
  opposes calcium-driven contraction.
- **Glucose metabolism, protein synthesis, bone structure and cardiac rhythm.**

**Sweat loss.** Small, typically under 1 mmol/L. Sweat magnesium is not a meaningful
route of loss in healthy people eating normally.

**Requirement.** 400–420 mg/day for men and 310–320 mg/day for women. The supplemental
upper limit is 350 mg/day, above which diarrhoea becomes likely. High-dose magnesium is
dangerous in kidney failure.

## Calcium (Ca²⁺)

**Where it is.** 99% in bone; the extracellular fraction is tightly regulated because it
signals so many processes.

**What it does.**

- **Excitation-contraction coupling.** Calcium entry into muscle cells triggers
  contraction; calcium reuptake allows relaxation.
- **Neurotransmitter release, hormone secretion and blood clotting.**
- **Bone mineralisation**, with vitamin D and phosphate.

**Sweat loss.** Small, typically under 1 mmol/L. Sweat calcium is not a reason to choose
one drink over another.

**Requirement.** 1,000 mg/day for most adults, rising to 1,200 mg/day for women over 50
and men over 70. The upper limit is 2,000–2,500 mg/day depending on age. Dietary calcium
is preferred; high-dose supplements have been associated with a modest cardiovascular
signal that remains contested.

## Chloride (Cl⁻)

**Where it is.** The main extracellular anion, following sodium to preserve
electroneutrality.

**What it does.**

- **Maintains osmolality and acid-base balance.**
- **Forms gastric hydrochloric acid.**
- **Supports the chloride shift** that lets red blood cells carry carbon dioxide.
- **Is required for sweat production**: the sweat gland secretes a sodium-chloride
  precursor and reabsorbs what it can.

**Sweat loss.** Substantial: about 32 mmol/L in marathoners, tracking sodium
([PH-05](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Because chloride is the anion that accompanies
sodium, replacing sodium chloride replaces both. This is why "chloride-free" sodium
products are physiologically odd, and why chloride is the nutrient labels most often
omit.

**Requirement.** Adequate intake 2,300 mg/day; upper limit 3,600 mg/day. Ordinary salted
food covers it easily.

## Why sodium dominates hydration advice

Sweat is dilute relative to plasma but sodium-dominant relative to every other
electrolyte. The approximate composition of sweat, and how it compares with a typical
sports drink and with ORS, explains most of the product landscape:

| Fluid | Sodium mmol/L | Potassium mmol/L | Chloride mmol/L | Osmolarity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sweat | 10–90 (mean ~42) | 4–8 | 20–60 | Hypotonic |
| Gatorade-class sports drink | ~20 | ~3 | ~10 | ~300 mOsm/L |
| WHO reduced-osmolarity ORS | 75 | 20 | 65 | 245 mOsm/L |
| Plasma | 135–145 | 4–5 | 100–110 | ~290 mOsm/L |

Sweat is hypotonic, so replacing it litre-for-litre with plain water dilutes plasma
sodium slightly. That is normally harmless because the kidneys excrete the excess water.
It becomes dangerous only when intake exceeds excretion, the mechanism of
exercise-associated hyponatraemia.

## What this means practically

1. **Sodium is the electrolyte worth paying for** when the goal is replacing sweat.
2. **Potassium matters for blood pressure over years**, not for an afternoon workout.
   Sweat potassium losses are small, and a normal meal replaces them.
3. **Magnesium and calcium in sports drinks are marketing**, at doses far below daily
   requirements and far below the amounts that would change anything.
4. **Chloride comes with sodium** when the sodium salt is sodium chloride. Products using
   sodium citrate deliver sodium without chloride; that is fine for hydration but is not
   a complete sweat replacement.
5. **Food is an electrolyte product.** A meal with salt, vegetables, dairy and fruit
   replaces everything sweat loses, usually more completely than a stick pack.
