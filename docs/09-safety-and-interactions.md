# Safety: excess sodium, excess potassium and medication interactions

Electrolyte products are sold as benign. Mostly they are. But the two electrolytes that
matter most are also the two with real toxicity when the kidneys or the drugs that act on
them are not normal. This chapter separates the risks that are real from the ones that are
marketing fiction.

## Sodium: the chronic risk, not the acute one

**Blood pressure.** Reducing sodium intake lowers blood pressure in a dose-dependent way.
A meta-analysis of 133 trials found a mean reduction of **4.26/2.07 mmHg** for a 130 mmol
reduction in 24-hour urinary sodium, with effects in both hypertensive and normotensive
people ([SA-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). In DASH-Sodium, the combination of the
DASH diet and 1,500 mg sodium lowered systolic pressure by **11.5 mmHg** in people with
hypertension and **7.1 mmHg** in those without, compared with a typical American diet at
3,300 mg ([SA-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

**Cardiovascular outcomes.** The evidence is messier than the blood-pressure evidence, but
it points the same way at high intake. Long-term follow-up of the Trials of Hypertension
Prevention found about a 25% lower cardiovascular event rate in the sodium-reduction arms
([SA-04](generated/evidence-tables.md)). PURE found a J-shaped association with lowest risk
at 3–5 g/day ([SA-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)), but its spot-urine method
systematically distorts the low end; repeated 24-hour collections show a graded
relationship instead. The J-curve is largely a measurement artefact.

**Salt substitutes.** The strongest outcome trial in the field replaced 25% of sodium
chloride with potassium chloride in 600 villages and reduced stroke by 14% (RR 0.86), with
no increase in serious hyperkalaemia events ([SA-05](generated/evidence-tables.md)). That
is a population intervention, not a licence for individuals with kidney disease to use
potassium salt.

**Kidney.** High sodium intake raises blood pressure and albuminuria and may accelerate
CKD progression; sodium restriction lowers blood pressure and proteinuria in CKD
([R65](../docs/references.md)). KDIGO recommends sodium restriction in CKD with
hypertension.

**What this means for supplements.** A 1,000 mg sodium stick contains about **2.5 g of
salt**: roughly half the WHO daily limit and about two-thirds of the CDRR. One is not a
problem for a healthy, active person. Four a day, on top of a normal diet, is a meaningful
sodium load. The risk is cumulative, not acute.

**Gastric cancer.** High salt intake is associated with gastric cancer, particularly in
populations with high baseline intake and *H. pylori* infection. This is a chronic dietary
effect, not a reason to avoid a sports drink.

## Potassium: the risk is excretion, not intake

Potassium is the electrolyte most likely to be dangerous, and almost never in the people
who worry about it.

**Healthy kidneys** excrete surplus potassium efficiently. There is no tolerable upper
intake level for potassium in healthy adults, and high dietary intake does not cause
hyperkalaemia ([SB-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Higher intake lowers blood pressure
and is associated with a 24% lower stroke risk ([SB-01](generated/evidence-tables.md),
[SB-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

**Impaired excretion is the danger.** Hyperkalaemia can be fatal through cardiac
arrhythmia. Risk rises sharply with:

- chronic kidney disease, especially stage G4–G5,
- heart failure,
- adrenal insufficiency,
- ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI, spironolactone and other mineralocorticoid receptor
  antagonists,
- trimethoprim, heparin, tacrolimus, NSAIDs,
- potassium-sparing diuretics,
- type 1 diabetes and hypoaldosteronism.

In these groups, even dietary potassium can be dangerous, and potassium-containing salt
substitutes should be avoided unless a clinician says otherwise
([SB-04](generated/evidence-tables.md), [R42](../docs/references.md),
[R43](../docs/references.md)).

**The 99 mg rule.** FDA restricts over-the-counter potassium *drug* products to 99 mg
elemental potassium per dose because higher doses of potassium chloride tablets caused
small-bowel lesions ([SB-05](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Dietary supplements are not
bound by the same limit, which is why a 700 mg potassium drink can be sold legally. The
rule is about local gastrointestinal injury from concentrated potassium chloride, not about
systemic safety in healthy people.

**Practical guidance.** If you have kidney disease, heart failure, or take any drug that
raises potassium, treat high-potassium electrolyte products as a medication question. Ask
before you drink them.

## Magnesium

Oral magnesium is poorly absorbed; the unabsorbed fraction draws water into the bowel and
causes diarrhoea. The supplemental upper limit is **350 mg/day**
([SC-02](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Magnesium is cleared by the kidneys, so
hypermagnesaemia occurs in kidney failure, sometimes severely, with hypotension,
bradycardia and respiratory depression.

A 300 mg magnesium tablet marketed for sleep or cramps is at the upper limit before any
food or other supplement is counted. It is not a sweat-replacement dose and does not belong
in a hydration product.

## Calcium

High-dose calcium supplements have been associated with a modest increase in myocardial
infarction risk (RR 1.31 in one meta-analysis; [SC-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). The
finding is contested, and dietary calcium is not implicated. The upper limit is
2,000–2,500 mg/day. Calcium in a sports drink is a trace amount, not a risk; calcium
supplements taken for bone health are a different decision.

## Chloride

Chloride is generally safe in food and fluids. The adequate intake is 2,300 mg/day and the
upper limit 3,600 mg/day. High chloride intake usually comes with high sodium intake, so
the sodium risk dominates. Metabolic acidosis from excessive chloride (hyperchloraemic
acidosis) is a clinical issue with large volumes of intravenous saline, not with drinks.

## Medication interactions

| Medication or class | Interaction | Practical advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI | Raise serum potassium | Avoid high-potassium salts; monitor potassium |
| Spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone | Raise potassium | Same; do not use potassium-based salt substitutes |
| Thiazide and loop diuretics | Lower potassium, lower sodium | Electrolytes may be needed; clinician sets the dose |
| Potassium-sparing diuretics | Raise potassium | Avoid potassium supplements |
| NSAIDs | Reduce renal potassium excretion, cause sodium retention | Caution with high sodium and potassium loads |
| Lithium | Sodium and water changes alter lithium levels | Keep sodium and fluid intake stable; dehydration can cause toxicity |
| Digoxin | Potassium abnormalities increase toxicity | Avoid large swings in potassium |
| Corticosteroids | Sodium retention, potassium loss | Monitor blood pressure and potassium |
| SGLT2 inhibitors | Mild osmotic diuresis, small electrolyte changes | Usually safe; watch volume status |
| Trimethoprim | Raises potassium | Avoid potassium supplements |
| Antidiarrhoeals + ORS | ORS is the treatment; antidiarrhoeals may be contraindicated | Follow clinical advice |

**Lithium deserves a special note.** Lithium clearance falls with dehydration and with
sodium restriction. An athlete on lithium who sweats heavily and drinks low-sodium fluid
can develop lithium toxicity. Stable sodium and fluid intake matters more than any
"optimal hydration" target.

## Who should not use these products without advice

- Chronic kidney disease, dialysis, or a kidney transplant.
- Heart failure or a history of hyperkalaemia.
- Adrenal insufficiency.
- Anyone taking an ACE inhibitor, ARB, MRA, trimethoprim, or lithium.
- Pregnancy with hypertension or pre-eclampsia.
- Children under 12, except ORS for diarrhoea under clinical guidance.
- People on a potassium-restricted diet.
- Anyone with a history of exercise-associated hyponatraemia (they need a fluid plan, not
  more salt).

## The risk that is actually common

The most frequent serious harm in this field is not sodium toxicity. It is
**exercise-associated hyponatraemia** from drinking too much hypotonic fluid, covered in
the [next chapter](10-dehydration-and-overhydration.md). Electrolyte products do not
prevent it, and a high-sodium drink can create a false sense of protection while the
athlete continues to overdrink.
