# Verdict and recommendations

## The one-paragraph answer

Electrolyte supplements are useful in a narrow, identifiable set of situations: prolonged
exercise, heat, high sweat losses, and illness with diarrhoea. Outside those situations,
water and food are sufficient, and the marketing around "electrolytes" mostly sells
flavour, convenience and a story. Sodium is the only electrolyte that matters acutely.
Potassium matters for blood pressure over years, not for a workout. Magnesium, calcium and
chloride in sports drinks are either irrelevant at the dose or already covered by food. The
real risks are not sodium deficiency in athletes; they are overdrinking (hyponatraemia) and
potassium excess in people with kidney disease or potassium-raising medication.

## Who benefits

**Definitely:**

- Endurance athletes exercising longer than about 2 hours, especially in heat.
- Heavy sweaters with visible salt stains and high measured sweat sodium.
- People doing hot physical work for hours (construction, farming, military, firefighting).
- Anyone rehydrating after a large sweat deficit who cannot eat a meal immediately.
- People with diarrhoea, with ORS, not a sports drink.
- Athletes who struggle to drink enough plain water because it tastes flat.

**Probably:**

- Team-sport athletes in hot conditions with limited drink breaks.
- People new to heat who have not yet acclimatised.
- Older adults with blunted thirst during prolonged heat exposure.

**Not:**

- Someone doing a 45-minute gym session in air conditioning.
- Someone doing yoga, walking, or light yard work.
- Anyone whose diet already contains 3–4 g sodium per day and who is not sweating heavily.
- Anyone with CKD, heart failure, hyperkalaemia, or on potassium-raising drugs, without
  clinical advice.
- Anyone trying to treat cramps with salt.

## What to ignore

- "Cellular hydration", "electrolyte balance", "bioelectrical impedance" marketing.
- Potassium doses above about 200 mg presented as hydration.
- Magnesium and calcium in sports drinks as a reason to buy them.
- "Salt tablets prevent heat illness."
- "If you're thirsty, you're already dehydrated."
- "Drink eight glasses a day."
- Sweat tests that promise a precise milligram prescription.
- Alkaline water claims.
- Proprietary blends.

## The evidence gaps

1. **No long-term outcome trials of electrolyte supplements** in athletes. The evidence is
   physiological and short-term.
2. **Sweat testing validation is weak.** Patch tests are not standardised and have
   substantial error.
3. **Cramp prevention** has no well-powered trial of sodium in a real-world setting.
4. **Individual sodium requirements** are poorly predicted by any measurable characteristic.
5. **Sweetener safety** is unsettled at the margins, particularly erythritol.
6. **Hyponatraemia prevention** works in principle (do not overdrink), but behavioural
   interventions are understudied.

## The practical bottom line

If you sweat heavily, exercise long, or train in heat: **weigh yourself, drink to thirst,
include sodium when losses are large, eat carbohydrate when the session is long, and do not
drink more than you lose.** A $0.40 sachet of ORS, a $0.93 scoop of Re-Lyte, or a bottle of
sports drink all fit that plan. The expensive stick is a convenience, not a pharmacological
upgrade.

If you do not sweat heavily and exercise for less than an hour: **water is enough.** The
best electrolyte supplement is a meal.

## Confidence in this review

High confidence: sweat composition, sodium physiology, ORS efficacy, blood-pressure effects
of sodium and potassium, hyponatraemia mechanism, the 99 mg potassium rule.

Moderate confidence: performance effects of sodium and carbohydrate, cost rankings (prices
move), product label values (not independently assayed).

Low confidence: cramp prevention, sweat-test-guided dosing, long-term outcomes of
electrolyte supplementation, sweetener safety at the margins.

These are stated so the reader can weigh the recommendations accordingly. Where the
evidence is weak, this review says so rather than filling the gap with a mechanism.
