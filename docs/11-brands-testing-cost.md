# Brands, transparency, testing and cost

## The market in one paragraph

Electrolyte products are cheap to manufacture and expensive to market. The ingredients,
sodium chloride, sodium citrate, potassium chloride, citric acid, sweetener, flavour,
cost cents per serving. What separates a $0.40 ORS sachet from a $1.50 premium stick is
branding, format, flavour and the story attached to the sodium. That is not automatically
a bad thing: convenience and palatability have real value, and someone has to pay for the
sachet, the design and the shelf space. But the consumer should know which part of the
price is electrolyte and which part is marketing.

## Transparency tiers

**Tier 1: full quantitative disclosure.** Every electrolyte is declared in milligrams,
the serving volume is stated, and the ingredient list explains the sources. Examples:
Nuun Sport (all five, verified against the NIH label database), SaltStick, Redmond Re-Lyte,
Hammer Endurolytes, TRIORAL (formula declared by mass and mmol/L).

**Tier 2: sodium and potassium declared, others omitted.** The common pattern. Gatorade,
Powerade, Liquid I.V., DripDrop, Skratch, Precision PH 1000, NormaLyte, BIOLYTE. The
omitted nutrients are usually present in small amounts, but the consumer cannot tell.

**Tier 3: "electrolytes" without amounts.** CORE Hydration is the clearest case: 0 mg
sodium declared, minerals listed as ingredients only. This is legal because "electrolytes"
is not a regulated nutrient claim in the way "good source of potassium" is. It is also
uninformative.

A brand that discloses more is not necessarily better formulated, but it is easier to
evaluate. Transparency is a proxy for accountability, not for quality.

## Independent testing and certification

Three programmes matter:

**NSF Certified for Sport.** Tests for more than 290 banned substances, plus label
accuracy and contaminants, on a lot-by-lot basis. It is the standard for professional and
collegiate athletes. In this database, Precision PH 1000 holds it; PRIME Hydration and
DripDrop Zero appear in NSF listings for some products and sizes
([product sources](generated/product-sources.md)).

**Informed Sport.** The other major banned-substance programme, widely used by UK and
European brands. Certification is product- and batch-specific.

**Clean Label Project / Labdoor.** Consumer-facing testing of heavy metals and label
accuracy. Useful signals, but not equivalent to NSF or Informed Sport, and not
comprehensive.

### What certification does and does not mean

- It means the specific product and size was tested for the stated panel.
- It does **not** mean the brand's other products are certified.
- It does **not** mean the product is effective, well formulated, or good value.
- It does **not** replace a certificate of analysis for a specific lot.

Most electrolyte brands in this sample have **no certification we could verify**. That is
a finding about the category, not an accusation: certification is expensive, and most
electrolyte products are low-risk from a doping perspective. For a tested athlete, though,
it is the first filter.

### Contaminants

Electrolyte products are simple inorganic salts, so the contaminant risk is lower than for
protein powders, botanicals or fish oil. The realistic concerns are heavy metals from
mineral sources, microbial contamination in poorly controlled powder lines, and label
inaccuracy. A product that publishes a lot-specific certificate of analysis is easier to
trust than one that says "third-party tested" without showing the result.

## Price analysis

Prices are November 2026 list prices. The full ranking is in
[cost per useful sodium dose](generated/value-ranking.md).

| Tier | Cost per 1,000 mg sodium | Products | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Budget | $0.23–$1.16 | TRIORAL, Re-Lyte, SaltStick | ORS is the outlier; Re-Lyte is the best-value consumer powder |
| Mid | $1.50–$3.50 | LMNT, NormaLyte, Waterboy, Nuun, Liquid I.V., Skratch, Pedialyte, DripDrop, PH 1000 | You are paying for format and flavour |
| Premium | $4.39–$8.81 | Gatorade Zero, BIOLYTE, Pedialyte RTD, Powerade, Gatorade | Bottled water and packaging dominate |

The spread is **38×** between the cheapest and most expensive meaningful sodium dose. Even
within the premium tier, the difference between a $2.38 Gatorade and a $3.33 BIOLYTE is
small compared with the difference between either and a $0.40 ORS sachet.

### What you are actually paying for

- **Sodium chloride** costs roughly $0.50/kg. A 1,000 mg sodium dose uses about 2.5 g of
  salt, worth about $0.001.
- **Potassium chloride** is similarly cheap.
- **Flavour, sweetener, acid and packaging** cost more than the electrolytes.
- **Brand, marketing and retail margin** dominate the retail price.

This is not a scandal. It is how consumer products work. It does mean that "premium
electrolyte" is a marketing category, not a chemical one.

### The premium traps

1. **Paying for potassium.** A 700 mg potassium drink costs more per sodium dose and
   delivers the electrolyte you lost least. Bananas, potatoes and beans are cheaper.
2. **Paying for magnesium and calcium.** The doses are too small to matter and too small
   to replace a supplement.
3. **Paying for "clean" sodium.** Sodium chloride is sodium chloride. Himalayan salt
   contains trace minerals at irrelevant concentrations.
4. **Paying for a bottle.** Ready-to-drink products cost 4–30× more per milligram than
   powder. You are buying water and logistics.
5. **Paying for sugar-free when you need fuel.** In endurance events, removing
   carbohydrate can remove the performance benefit while keeping the premium price.

## Brand-by-brand notes

These are observations about formulation and transparency, not endorsements.

**LMNT.** High sodium (1,000 mg), no sugar, stevia, 200 mg potassium, 60 mg magnesium.
One of the few products that genuinely matches a heavy sweat loss per stick. Expensive per
milligram and designed for 16–32 oz, so concentration varies with how you mix it. No
certification verified.

**Liquid I.V.** 500–560 mg sodium, 370 mg potassium, 11–12 g sugar, stevia. Positioned as
"cellular hydration" with added vitamins. Good palatability, moderate sodium, high cost per
milligram. The potassium is disproportionate to sweat.

**Nuun Sport.** 300 mg sodium, 150 mg potassium, 25 mg magnesium, 13 mg calcium, 40 mg
chloride, 1 g sugar. The most transparent mainstream tablet and the best example of a
"light" electrolyte product. Costs $2.50 per 1,000 mg sodium; you need multiple tablets for
a heavy sweat loss.

**DripDrop.** 330 mg sodium in 8 oz (1,398 mg/L), 180 mg potassium, 39 mg magnesium, 7 g
sugar, sucralose. ORS-adjacent concentration in a consumer format. Good option for
moderate losses; expensive per milligram.

**Pedialyte.** The paediatric ORS brand. The classic powder is 490 mg sodium per 473 mL
with 570 mg chloride and 12 g sugar; the RTD is 390 mg per 360 mL. Clinically oriented,
widely available, and more expensive per milligram than generic ORS.

**Gatorade / Gatorade Zero.** 270 mg sodium per 20 oz, 80 mg potassium, 34 g sugar (or 2 g
in Zero). The reference sports drink: adequate sodium for moderate conditions, excellent
for carbohydrate, poor value per milligram. Zero is a hydration product, not a fueling
product.

**Powerade.** 400 mg sodium per 20 oz, 34 g sugar, high-fructose corn syrup. Slightly more
sodium than Gatorade, similar carbohydrate, similar value.

**BODYARMOR.** 30 mg sodium, 680 mg potassium, 25 g sugar. A potassium-forward sports drink,
not a sodium-replacement product. Good source of potassium; poor choice for a heavy
sweater.

**Skratch Labs.** 400 mg sodium, 50 mg each of potassium, magnesium and calcium, 19 g cane
sugar (3.8%). Hypotonic, no artificial sweetener, pleasant. A well-formulated light
sports drink; costs $2.99 per 1,000 mg sodium.

**Precision Fuel & Hydration.** 500 mg sodium per 500 mL sachet, 125 mg potassium, 12 mg
magnesium, 24 mg calcium, 16 g sugar, NSF Certified for Sport. The most evidence-oriented
brand in the sample, with a sweat-testing service. Expensive per serving but well
documented.

**SaltStick.** 215 mg sodium, 63 mg potassium, 11 mg magnesium, 22 mg calcium, 349 mg
chloride per capsule. The best-value capsule, and one of the few that declares chloride.
Useful when fluid volume is limited.

**Hammer Endurolytes.** 80 mg sodium per two capsules. Low sodium for the price; the
formulation is more about magnesium and calcium than sodium. Poor value as a sodium
product.

**Redmond Re-Lyte.** 810 mg sodium, 400 mg potassium, 50 mg magnesium, 60 mg calcium,
1,280 mg chloride, no sugar, stevia. The best-value high-sodium powder in the sample at
$0.93 per 1,000 mg. Potassium-heavy relative to sweat, but the sodium dose is real.

**CORE.** 0 mg sodium declared, minerals unquantified. Flavoured water. Not a sodium
replacement product.

**PRIME.** 10 mg sodium, 700 mg potassium, 124 mg magnesium. A potassium-forward lifestyle
drink. NSF Certified for Sport for some SKUs. Not a sweat-replacement product.

**Waterboy.** 1,120 mg sodium, 350 mg potassium, 50 mg magnesium, 1,685 mg chloride, no
sugar. The highest sodium concentration in the sample at 2,368 mg/L. Marketed for recovery;
works as a high-sodium stick if the concentration is tolerated.

**TRIORAL.** The WHO formula in a sachet: 1,725 mg sodium, 780 mg potassium, 13.5 g
glucose, 245 mOsm/L, $0.40 per sachet. The cheapest sodium in the database by a factor of
four. Unflavoured and salty; not a lifestyle product, but it is what the evidence supports
for serious rehydration.

**NormaLyte.** 851 mg sodium, 393 mg potassium, 6.75 g dextrose, WHO-aligned concentration.
A more palatable ORS-style stick at $1.57 per 1,000 mg.

**BIOLYTE.** 700 mg sodium, 400 mg potassium, 12 g carbohydrate, monk fruit and stevia, plus
a proprietary blend. Premium bottled recovery drink; $4.76 per 1,000 mg.

## How to choose without overthinking it

1. Decide whether you need **sodium** (sweat replacement), **carbohydrate** (fuel), or
   **both**.
2. Check sodium per serving and per litre against your estimated sweat loss.
3. Check carbohydrate percentage against your fueling plan.
4. Check whether the label declares what it claims.
5. Check certification if you are drug-tested.
6. Divide cost by sodium per serving. That is the value number.

Everything else is taste.
